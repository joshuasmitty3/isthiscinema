title: nolans achilles heel
date: 2026-09-07
---

When you watch a Nolan movie, you already know what you're in for. Watch it in theaters, in its proper state. The closer to opening night, the better. Did he use CGI or shoot everything practical? Did he invent some new lighting rig or camera lens or elaborate set design? Who was even allowed to read the script? Wait... is the movie's runtime an easter egg?

We know he writes the script and then scrambles the order, Pulp Fiction style. Telling a story in order, one scene after another, is sacrilege. The story has to be told backwards, or in thematic chunks, or interspliced with parallel storylines, across multiple time periods, all at once. The pacing is perpetually "meanwhile back at the ranch."

The final Nolan-ism we look for is, coincidentally, M. Night Shyamalan-ism. Every movie must have a twist. The point of his elaborate pacing is to set up a reveal, a cinematic ta-da. He embeds a theme throughout, and like breadcrumbs, each scene connects to the next only until we're ready for the third act, and then, moments before the credits roll, the reveal snaps into focus. When well executed, when he sticks the landing, and he doesn't always, it really works. His endings are near perfect. No fat. Boom, then credits. The entire movie is reevaluated and feels cathartic.

However, it's this catharsis that draws Nolan closer to Michael Bay and Zack Snyder than Kubrick or Spielberg in my opinion. At some point, he got addicted to that dopamine hit. *Intensitymaxxing.* Filmmakers so obsessed with climaxes that they forget the foreplay. A simple scene where two characters talk and it feels natural and organic, where maybe some tension or nuanced emotion emerges, is an impossibility. The only way to show emotion is a father on a spaceship light years away weeping as he watches tapes of his "now" grown kids.

Because The Odyssey, like other Nolan films, slowly builds to its climax, it could be mistaken for being patient, even thoughtful at times, but the gravitational pull of the impending twist is so strong, so obviously set up, that every building block screams that it's a plot point. No more, no less. There's nothing frivolous, no character quirks, no jokes, no breathing room between scenes or even within scenes. There is only plot. Nolan's all-seeing, all-knowing plot. It marches forward, to the beat of Hans Zimmer. Perfect and relentless. There is no space, dare I say, no jazz? And without space, there isn't any room for interpretation, at least not beyond Nolan's singular vision.

The other side effect of his plot-heavy approach is that there's little visual language. For a filmmaker who promotes celluloid-packed film stocks and theatrical experiences, there isn't much beauty in The Odyssey. There's epicness, there's spectacle, there's behind-the-scenes awareness of practicals over CGI, but if you froze any frame and looked at it as though it were a photograph, you'd see nothing noteworthy. You don't even need to watch the movie. Watch [this trailer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mzw2ttJD2qQ), then watch [this one](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NdvqHc56lE0). Colors, composition, lighting, framing, anything visually compelling beyond the action? Same critique applies to Tarantino. For filmmakers who push the transcendent experience of sitting in a dark space waiting to dream, they sure do have odd blind spots imagery. I've seen more visual flair in crypto commercials.

Fincher once said he likes to move the camera as though it has some omniscient awareness of what's going to happen. Removed from the characters' motivations, their struggles and desires, it pans and zooms and dollies and cuts with detachment. It already knows what will happen and knows there's no way for the characters to realize it. They're in a Greek tragedy, and only they don't know it.

Still his best movie since Interstellar. **4/5**
